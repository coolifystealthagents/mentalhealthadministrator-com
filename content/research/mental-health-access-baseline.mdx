An access baseline should describe what a practice can observe without pretending its records measure total community need. National prevalence gives population context. Local inquiries describe people who reached a channel. Appointment starts describe documented service activity. These quantities have different populations, and dividing one by another can produce a persuasive but meaningless percentage.

This brief provides a nonclinical measurement design. It does not diagnose unmet need, predict demand, establish provider adequacy, or judge clinical eligibility. Qualified staff remain responsible for suitability, urgency, and level-of-care decisions.

## Start with a question and geography

A baseline can support capacity planning, channel evaluation, referral follow-up, or equity review. State which decision is intended. Then identify service lines, locations, appointment modes, reporting dates, and the population actually visible to the systems. A website form may cover a different geography from a contracted payer directory.

External sources such as NIMH, SAMHSA, and HRSA can frame prevalence, service use, and workforce context. Retain the source year, survey population, estimate definition, and geographic level. Do not apply a national percentage directly to a local catchment or treat a shortage designation as a forecast for one office.

> **Liftable statistic:** NIMH reported that 59.3 million U.S. adults had any mental illness in 2022. The estimate concerns a defined national adult population and year; it is not the number seeking a particular practice's services.

## Build an observable access funnel

Use stable inquiry IDs and timestamped events. A six-stage local model can distinguish:

1. inquiry received through a monitored channel;
2. administrative contact established;
3. required intake information available;
4. authorized eligibility or fit decision recorded;
5. compatible appointment offered and accepted;
6. first service start observed in the scheduling or encounter system.

Not every workflow uses all stages, and clinical decisions should enter only as attributed outcomes. Preserve original dates when a case reopens. If one person makes duplicate inquiries, report both inquiry volume and deduplicated people using a declared matching method. Keep uncertain duplicates rather than merging on name alone.

Create loss categories at each transition: unable to contact, person withdrew, referred elsewhere by an authorized decision, no compatible capacity, insurance or payment issue reported, duplicate, and unknown. These are attributed administrative facts, not explanations of motive.

## Denominators and companion measures

A funnel conversion uses records reaching the next stage divided by records eligible to reach it. Records near the reporting cutoff may not have had sufficient observation time. Either use a mature cohort with a fixed follow-up period or show right-censored records as pending. A monthly snapshot that counts starts but includes brand-new inquiries in the denominator will vary with timing.

Useful measures include:

* unique inquiries by received week and channel;
* proportion with a documented contact result among inquiries with enough follow-up;
* median and distribution of inquiry-to-first-offer time among records receiving an offer;
* compatible slots released by service, mode, and location;
* offer acceptance count and decline categories;
* service starts divided by accepted bookings with adequate follow-up;
* unresolved records in aging bands at period end.

A wait among those eventually booked excludes people who never received an offer. Publish that limitation. Pair demand and supply by compatible category rather than comparing all inquiries with all calendar openings.

## Data collection and validation

Freeze a data dictionary before extraction. For every field, specify source system, allowable values, event timestamp, later-edit behavior, and owner. Retain raw exports and document joins among inquiry, scheduling, referral, and encounter systems. Use one time zone and define how daylight-saving changes are handled.

Validate the funnel in both directions. Sample inquiries and trace forward through every stage. Then sample first appointments and trace backward to see whether they originated through captured channels. The backward sample detects walk-ins, clinician-created bookings, and migrated records absent from the inquiry system.

Check missingness by channel and location. If telephone outcomes are less structured than portal outcomes, a combined completeness rate can merely reflect channel mix. Report system outages, new forms, campaign periods, and service closures alongside the time series.

## Segmenting access responsibly

Stratification can reveal where to investigate, but subgroup fields require quality checks and privacy protection. Use self-reported language, communication request, location, age category where authorized, payer category, or referral source only when definitions are stable. Include unknown values. Suppressing unknowns can make a dashboard look more equitable while removing the least complete records.

Do not infer protected characteristics. Avoid publishing small cells that permit identification. A difference between groups is descriptive. It may reflect channel availability, capacity, referral patterns, missingness, service mix, or other factors. Causal and discrimination claims need stronger designs and expertise.

## Comparing periods

For a baseline, choose a period long enough to include ordinary variability but report seasonal context. Compare like windows and keep the metric version fixed. When definitions change, calculate an overlap period under both versions if possible. Mark staff changes, new payer participation, directory updates, and new appointment types.

A simple before-and-after chart cannot isolate the effect of an intervention. If leaders want to evaluate a change, preregister the primary metric, comparison approach, implementation date, exclusions, and competing changes. Retain absolute counts because percentages can rise when the denominator shrinks.

## Limitations

People who never contact the practice are unobserved. Inquiry systems may miss direct clinician contacts or third-party portals. One person can appear more than once, while aggressive matching can combine different people. Service start is not the same as continued care or beneficial outcome. External survey definitions and years may not align with local categories.

Capacity is also multidimensional. A nominal opening may not match age range, modality, location, schedule, payer, or authorized clinical needs. Administrative data should not be used to second-guess a qualified fit decision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can prevalence estimate how many calls the practice should receive?

Not directly. Prevalence and practice inquiries have different populations, periods, geography, and service-seeking behavior. Use prevalence only as clearly labeled context.

### What is the best single access metric?

There is no universal one. A compact set should include volume, transition counts, time distributions, unresolved aging, and compatible supply. Each answers a different operational question.

### Should duplicate inquiries be removed?

Report inquiry events and deduplicated people separately. Publish the matching rule and uncertain count. Repeated contact may itself identify a workflow issue.

### Does a booked appointment prove access?

It proves a booking event. Attendance, service start, suitability, continuity, and outcome require separate evidence.

## Conclusion

A credible access baseline separates national context from local observation and makes every funnel transition explicit. Mature cohorts, compatible supply categories, bidirectional validation, and visible unknowns produce a useful operational picture without claiming to measure all need or clinical success.
