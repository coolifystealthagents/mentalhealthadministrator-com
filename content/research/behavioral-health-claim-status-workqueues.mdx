A claim can be accepted by a clearinghouse and still be rejected by a payer. It can be adjudicated and later adjusted. A payment can cover several claims, while one claim can produce multiple service-line outcomes. Claim status workqueues become unreliable when these distinct events are collapsed into submitted, pending, or done.

This research record presents an administrative data model for traceable follow-up. It does not tell a practice which code to use, whether a service is covered, whether an appeal is warranted, or what a client owes.

## Scope and observation units

Use the claim version as the operational unit, with service lines nested beneath it and transactions linked around it. Preserve the original claim ID, payer control number, clearinghouse trace, submission batch, replacement or void relationship, and remittance reference where available. Never recycle a local identifier when a corrected claim is sent.

Define the workqueue population by a concrete trigger, such as payer rejection, no qualifying response by a locally declared checkpoint, denial on remittance, or unmatched payment. Exclude test claims and confirmed duplicates, but publish counts. Claims still inside an ordinary response window should not be labeled overdue merely to enlarge the queue.

> **Liftable statistic:** CMS guidance states that Medicare fee-for-service claims generally must be filed no later than 12 months after the date of service. That scope does not create a universal deadline for Medicaid, commercial plans, corrected claims, appeals, or contract-specific requirements.

## Seven event families

A practical ledger distinguishes created, transmitted, intermediary accepted or rejected, payer accepted or rejected, adjudicated, remitted, and posted or reconciled. Local labels should never overwrite source language. If a payer portal and electronic response conflict, store both observations with their capture times and route the conflict.

Pending must identify the awaited event and responsible party. For example, awaiting payer adjudication is different from awaiting clinician documentation, coding review, enrollment correction, or bank reconciliation. The queue should expose that distinction so staff do not repeatedly contact the wrong party.

Closure criteria depend on queue type. A rejected-submission item may close when a corrected version is accepted by the payer. A denied-line item may close when an authorized owner records a disposition. A remittance item may close after payment and contractual adjustments reconcile to posting. None of these automatically means the underlying service was payable.

## Workqueue fields and routing

Minimum fields include claim version, service line when applicable, event that opened the item, raw reason code, plain-language local category, source timestamp, received timestamp, owner, next permitted action, next review date, and closure evidence. Store portal screenshots or response files in an approved repository rather than an uncontrolled tracker.

Route by actionable cause, not by dollar value alone. Data-format rejection, enrollment mismatch, missing source document, coding question, payer processing delay, and unmatched remittance require different owners. Administrative staff can collect and transmit existing facts, but coding, clinical documentation, appeal rationale, and financial decisions stay with authorized personnel.

## Measures

Queue inflow equals newly opened items during the period. Queue outflow equals items meeting their declared closure rule during the period. Ending inventory should reconcile as opening inventory plus inflow minus outflow, adjusted only for documented merges or deletions.

Report age bands from the opening event, with paused time shown separately if the procedure permits pauses. Useful measures include event-mapping completeness, items without owners, repeat rejection after resubmission, reopened items, unmatched remittances, and source-to-ledger agreement in an audit sample. Denominators should be claim versions or service lines consistently, never a mixture.

A financial amount may be useful for prioritization, but totals can change through replacement claims, line adjudication, recoupment, or payment allocation. Label amounts by source and date. Do not present submitted charges as expected revenue.

## Reconciliation and quality testing

Trace samples in both directions. Start with queue items and locate their raw transaction evidence. Then start with payer rejections, remittances, and adjustments and confirm that qualifying exceptions reached the queue. The second test catches missing interfaces that a queue-only review cannot see.

Test all local mappings added or changed during the period. Inspect impossible sequences, such as posted before remitted, and duplicate closure events. Review items closed by automation separately from staff closures. Preserve late-arriving events after the reporting freeze as a runout appendix rather than rewriting the original snapshot.

## Limitations

Payer portals, clearinghouses, electronic transactions, and bank records may update at different times. A missing response can reflect an interface failure rather than payer inaction. Reason codes do not always identify root cause, and staff-selected categories introduce classification error. Results from one payer mix or billing platform do not generalize automatically. A queue can measure administrative follow-up but cannot prove coding correctness, coverage, payment entitlement, or client liability.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does clearinghouse acceptance mean the payer accepted the claim?

No. Record intermediary and payer acceptance separately, using each source's transaction reference and timestamp.

### When should a pending claim enter a workqueue?

Use a declared trigger based on the expected event, source, payer context, and local procedure. Do not apply one unexplained age threshold to every claim.

### Can staff close a denial after resubmission?

Only if the queue's closure definition is resubmission. If the purpose is final reconciliation, resubmission is an intermediate event and the item remains open or linked to a successor.

### How should later recoupments appear?

Append a new adjustment or recoupment event and reopen or link the relevant item under the procedure. Do not erase the earlier remittance and posting history.

## Conclusion

A trustworthy claim workqueue is an event ledger with explicit triggers, owners, and closure evidence. Separating acceptance, adjudication, remittance, and posting prevents operational shorthand from becoming a false payment claim. Bidirectional reconciliation and runout reporting make missing and late evidence visible.
