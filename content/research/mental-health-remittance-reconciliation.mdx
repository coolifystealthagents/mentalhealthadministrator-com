Remittance reconciliation asks whether money and explanations can be followed across systems. It is not just payment posting. One electronic remittance may cover many claims, one bank deposit may combine transactions, and later adjustments can alter earlier entries. This brief presents a nonclinical control method for linking the remittance, deposit, claim, and account ledger without making coding, refund, contractual, or patient-responsibility decisions.

## Scope and transaction grain

Retain three linked grains: remittance header, claim payment segment, and service line. Add a deposit or electronic funds transfer reference and the local posting batch. Aggregating immediately to payer or month hides partial matches and duplicate activity. Stable source identifiers should remain unchanged even if a local system assigns another ID.

The eligible population for a monthly review is all remittance headers received in the period plus deposits that the bank source identifies as payer receipts. Include zero-payment remittances in operational review because they can carry adjustment information even though they have no deposit match. Label reversals and corrected remittances rather than netting them away.

## Four-leg reconciliation model

The proposed model links four evidence legs. The remittance leg supplies payer trace information, claim references, paid amounts, and adjustment codes. The cash leg supplies settlement date, bank amount, and deposit reference. The claim leg supplies the submitted claim and version. The ledger leg shows how the transaction was posted locally.

> **Liftable statistic:** This editorial method proposes 4 local evidence legs for reconciliation: remittance, cash, claim, and ledger. Four is a control-design count, not a federal requirement or proof that an account balance is correct.

Create a match table with source IDs, source amounts, currency, receipt dates, match method, confidence state, worker or process ID, and exception ID. Permit one-to-many and many-to-one relationships. Do not force an exact single-record match when a deposit legitimately combines remittances.

## Measures and denominators

Header match rate is remittance headers linked to a cash event or valid zero-payment classification divided by eligible remittance headers. Deposit match rate reverses the question: payer deposits linked to one or more remittance headers divided by eligible payer deposits. These rates require different denominators and should not be merged.

Posting completeness is service lines with a recorded local posting outcome divided by eligible remittance service lines. Source agreement compares the posted amount and codes with the remittance, allowing declared transformation rules. Neither measure determines whether the submitted code, payer handling, write off, refund, or remaining balance is substantively correct.

Exception age begins when the mismatch is first detected. Report the median and age bands with counts, not only an average. Pause rules must be declared. Preserve the original detection timestamp when an exception moves between teams. Reopened items should retain prior closure and receive a new interval.

## Exception taxonomy

Useful administrative categories include missing remittance, unmatched deposit, amount mismatch, duplicate remittance, duplicate posting, reversal awaiting action, unknown claim reference, provider identifier mismatch, posting interface failure, and decision required. The last category needs a named authorized destination such as coding, contracting, compliance, or finance.

Store the payer's exact code and description where permitted, then separately record the local routing category. A payer code is source evidence, not permission for an assistant to infer medical necessity, contractual treatment, or patient responsibility. Free-text notes should explain the mismatch without copying unnecessary clinical information.

## Review procedure

Freeze source extracts and retain query parameters. Reconcile header totals first, then claim and service details. Test both directions so a clean remittance file does not conceal an unmatched bank receipt. Sample exact matches, automated matches, manual matches, zero payments, reversals, and aged exceptions.

For each sample, trace IDs back to source, recalculate amounts, inspect transformation rules, and verify the posting batch. Review access and change logs for manual overrides. When evidence conflicts, keep both source values and route the issue. Corrections should append a new event instead of erasing the original posting history.

## Failure modes

A clearinghouse can resend a file, producing a duplicate that looks new. A payer may combine settlements or use a trace number formatted differently from the bank. Interfaces can truncate identifiers. Staff may close an exception after routing it, even though the receiving owner has not decided it. Netting reversals against new payments can conceal sequence. Month-end cutoffs can make received, settled, and posted periods disagree.

Controls include duplicate fingerprints, normalized identifiers with raw values retained, explicit transferred and resolved states, reversible posting batches, and a cutoff bridge showing transactions that cross periods. Threshold-based auto-matching should have a versioned rule and sampled validation.

## Limitations

A matched amount can still reflect an incorrect claim or payer decision. Bank timing and remittance timing differ. Some payers and payment types may not use the same transaction path. Inaccessible clearinghouse logs can limit provenance. Local contract terms and account policies are outside this method. Trends may change with payer mix, volume, interfaces, staffing, or cutoff rules. The review cannot certify billing or regulatory compliance.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is an 835 proof that a balance is correct?

No. It communicates remittance information in an adopted transaction format. Balance treatment may require coding, contract, refund, compliance, or finance review.

### Should zero-payment remittances be excluded?

Not from workflow completeness. Classify them separately from cash matching because they may carry denials, adjustments, or other explanations.

### When may an exception be closed?

Use a defined closure event. Transfer to another owner is not resolution unless the metric explicitly measures routing rather than final reconciliation.

### Can automated matching replace sampling?

No. Sample automated matches and nonmatches. A stable software rule can repeat a mapping error consistently.

## Conclusion

Reliable remittance reconciliation preserves transaction grain and connects four independent evidence legs. Bidirectional matching, explicit zero-payment handling, durable exception age, and protected decision boundaries make mismatches visible without claiming that administrative agreement proves coding or financial correctness.
