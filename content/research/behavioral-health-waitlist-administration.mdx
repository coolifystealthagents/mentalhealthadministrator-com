A waitlist is not one line ordered by one date. It is an event history: a person enters, required administrative information arrives, an authorized reviewer may set a priority, staff make an offer, the person responds, and the record eventually leaves or pauses. A defensible review reconstructs those events instead of treating the latest status as the whole story.

This page addresses nonclinical queue administration. It does not determine urgency, diagnosis, treatment fit, level of care, or whether a particular person should move ahead. Those decisions belong to qualified personnel following the practice's approved policy. The cited sources frame access, communication, privacy, logging, and workforce constraints; the event model and fairness-audit method below are explicitly proposed local controls, not externally validated standards.

## Questions this review should answer

A useful waitlist study asks whether the written ordering rule was followed, whether people had a fair opportunity to respond, and whether records remained visible while unresolved. It does not ask administrators to invent reasons for delay. Before extraction, specify the service, location, age group if applicable, entry rule, time period, and statuses included.

Choose the unit deliberately. For demand, the unit may be one inquiry. For placement, it may be one person-service combination because one person can legitimately appear in different program queues. Document how duplicates, transfers, reopened cases, and requests for multiple clinicians are handled. Otherwise, apparent queue growth may be a change in record creation rather than demand.

## Minimum event model

Keep immutable events and derive the current state from them. A compact ledger can contain:

| Event | Required evidence | Frequent failure |
|---|---|---|
| Entry | queue ID, received timestamp, channel | referral date substituted for receipt |
| Ready | rule version, evidence complete time | readiness backdated after review |
| Priority instruction | authorized owner, exact category, effective time | administrator infers urgency |
| Offer | slot, mode, contact channel, attempt time | offer recorded without opportunity to reply |
| Response | accepted, declined, no response, requested later | silence labeled refusal |
| Exit or pause | reason, actor, date, review date if paused | record deleted from denominator |

Clinical text is usually unnecessary in an operational extract. A category issued by an authorized reviewer can be carried forward without copying the underlying narrative. Where two sources disagree, retain the conflict and its resolution rather than overwriting the earlier value.

> **Liftable statistic:** A proposed local waitlist audit uses 5 event dates: inquiry, readiness, offer, response, and disposition. This is a workflow control count, not a published benchmark or a clinical prioritization rule.

## Methodology for a fairness audit

Freeze the policy version and obtain an event-level export for a fixed period. Recreate the eligible queue immediately before each offered slot. Then compare the selected record with other records that were ready for the same service, format, location, and other approved administrative constraints. If priority categories exist, use only categories assigned by authorized staff.

Review every apparent out-of-order offer if volume permits. If sampling is necessary, stratify by location, service, month, and disposition, and oversample long waits without presenting the oversampled result as the overall rate. A second reviewer should independently classify ambiguous offer and pause events. Preserve both initial ratings and the adjudication.

Contact opportunity also needs review. Compare attempted channels with the person's recorded communication preference and permitted contact information. Delivery indicators show transmission, not comprehension. A disconnected number or returned message is evidence of an unsuccessful attempt, not proof that the person declined.

## Measures with explicit denominators

Use several measures because one average conceals different mechanisms.

* **Ready-to-first-offer days:** calendar days from the first ready timestamp to the first valid offer among records that reached ready status. Report the median and distribution, not only the mean.
* **Offer opportunity rate:** eligible records receiving at least one valid offer divided by records eligible during the window. State whether newly ready records near period end are excluded.
* **Unresolved aging:** open records in age bands divided by all open records at the snapshot. Snapshot results cannot describe records that already exited.
* **Ordering exception rate:** reviewed offers inconsistent with the documented rule divided by offers for which ordering could be reconstructed. Records lacking history belong in a separate unassessable count.
* **Administrative closure rate:** closures with the required reason and actor divided by all administrative closures. This measures documentation, not whether closure was appropriate.

Segment only where categories are reliably captured and privacy risk is acceptable. Differences by language request, communication channel, location, or payer may identify a process to investigate, but do not establish discrimination or causation on their own.

## Failure modes and corrective tests

A common failure is resetting the queue date after missing paperwork, which hides elapsed time before readiness. Keep both inquiry and ready dates. Another is marking a person unreachable after a single attempt even when the policy calls for a sequence; test attempt count and spacing against the policy in effect. Bulk status changes during migration can create false exits, so reconcile migrated IDs and inspect timestamps clustered at deployment.

Capacity changes complicate interpretation. A longer wait may reflect fewer compatible slots, more inquiries, a changed service definition, or altered data entry. Pair wait measures with offered slot counts by service and with policy version dates. Do not claim that a reminder, vendor, or staffing change caused improvement from a simple before-and-after comparison.

## Limitations

Event logs may omit telephone attempts, and staff may document after the event. People who never reached the formal queue are absent, so waitlist data cannot measure all unmet need. A queue snapshot overrepresents long-running records. Small groups create unstable percentages and reidentification risk. Local administrative categories are rarely comparable across organizations without mapping definitions.

The audit also cannot determine whether an offered appointment was clinically suitable, whether a person understood a message, or whether an outcome resulted from waiting. Those questions require different evidence and authorized expertise.

## Frequently asked questions

### Should the queue always be first come, first served?

Not necessarily. The practice may have authorized priority, program, or continuity rules. Administration should implement the documented rule and preserve who supplied any professional decision, not create a universal ordering rule.

### Is a declined offer the same as leaving the waitlist?

Only if the approved policy says so and the person's response supports that status. A person may decline one time while remaining interested. Store the offer response separately from final disposition.

### How should paused records be counted?

Define pause reasons and whether elapsed time continues for each measure. Show paused records as a separate count, assign a review date, and avoid removing them invisibly from both open and closed totals.

### Can average wait time compare two locations?

Only after checking service mix, entry rules, operating days, priority categories, censoring, and data completeness. Publish these differences and prefer distributions and matched categories over one unqualified average.

## Conclusion

Reliable waitlist administration depends on reconstructable events, not a polished current-state spreadsheet. Separate inquiry from readiness, offers from responses, and pauses from exits. Test actual ordering against the policy that applied, publish records that cannot be assessed, and keep every clinical priority decision with its authorized source.
