## Why the quarter start exposes renewal gaps

A prior-authorization renewal queue drifts fastest in the two weeks before a quarter starts. New referrals arrive while the front desk reconciles schedules, and renewals that looked comfortable in August now sit weeks from expiration. An audit at quarter start catches those items while there is still room to request an extension or move an appointment. Waiting for the payer notice turns a routine administrative task into a same-week scheduling problem.

The renewal queue also hides work that nobody actually owns. One coordinator may assume the billing team tracks expirations while the billing team assumes the clinician's office does. During a busy quarter change, that assumption becomes a missed visit. An audit makes the assumption visible by asking one question for each open authorization: who checks this next, and on what date?

Quarter boundaries matter because payers often reset benefit periods, accumulators, and review windows at the same time. A renewal that was valid under the old period may need a fresh clinical summary even when the authorization number has not changed. Reading the effective dates beside the appointment calendar prevents a valid-looking record from covering the wrong month. The audit is a date reconciliation exercise before it is a paperwork exercise.

## Define the queue's date fields

Most renewal confusion starts with two dates that look interchangeable. The expiration date tells the practice when coverage ends, while the visit date tells the practice when the service must be delivered. Sorting by expiration alone hides a session scheduled for the final covered day, when a same-day denial leaves no room to recover. Sort first by the date of the next scheduled session, then by expiration.

The queue should also record the date the payer last confirmed the authorization. A confirmation from six weeks ago may no longer reflect the current review status, especially if the practice submitted a request in between. Add a confirmed-on field and require a new entry after every payer contact. That single field separates a current approval from a stale note that only looks current.

Decide how the practice will treat a missing response from the payer. A pending request near the visit date is an exception, not a normal queue item, and it needs a scheduled call rather than another reminder. Write the escalation threshold as a number of business days before the visit. Every reviewer then applies the same rule instead of judging urgency by how worried the caller sounds.

## Separate clinical judgment from administrative prep

The administrative team can assemble the authorization packet, confirm the member identifier, and record the payer reference. It cannot decide whether the clinical record supports medical necessity or whether a reduced service level is acceptable. Keep those decisions with the clinician or the designated clinical reviewer, and route the file to that person by name. A clear boundary prevents a coordinator from writing language that sounds like a clinical opinion.

Preparation tasks and decisions need different status values. Use ready for clinical review, awaiting payer response, and scheduled follow-up rather than a single in progress label. When one status covers everything, the queue cannot show whether the delay belongs to the practice or the payer. A reviewer who can see the distinction knows which files to work first and which ones only need a check-in call.

Document the handoff when the file leaves the administrative queue. Record who received it, what question they are answering, and when the answer is expected back. The note should be short enough that a coordinator can write it in under a minute. Handoffs that live only in a hallway conversation disappear when the regular coordinator takes a day off.

## Run the audit in one afternoon

Pull every open authorization with a visit scheduled in the next ninety days. Divide the list into three groups: expiring within fourteen days, expiring within thirty days, and expiring later. Ask two staff members to review the first two groups independently, then compare their findings. A short overlap check catches the rows that one person reads as complete because they recognize the client name.

For each row, confirm four fields against the payer record: the authorization number, the covered service, the effective dates, and the units remaining. Correct the queue before changing anything else, and note the source of each correction. If a field cannot be confirmed, mark it unknown rather than copying the prior value forward. Unknown values are useful audit findings because they show where the practice has been guessing.

Time the exercise and write down how long each group took. The audit should be repeatable next quarter with roughly the same effort. If the first pass takes an entire day, the queue probably needs fewer fields or better filters. A practical audit produces a short list of fixes and a number the practice administrator can compare next time.

## Document exceptions with a named owner

Every exception needs an owner, a reason, and a next check date. An exception without those three elements is just an item that has been moved to a different list. Record whether the delay comes from missing clinical information, an unanswered payer request, or a scheduling conflict. That distinction decides who acts next and what evidence the practice will need later.

Do not let the same exception recur silently across quarters. Tag repeat exceptions and review the tags together at the end of the audit. Three renewals stuck for the same missing document is a process problem, not three unlucky files. Fix the intake step or the reminder rule before the next batch of authorizations reaches the same point.

Keep the exception note free of clinical interpretation. State the administrative fact, the person contacted, and the response received. If a clinician needs to make a determination, link to the request rather than paraphrasing the clinical reasoning. This keeps the administrative record useful during a payer review without blurring who made the clinical call.

## Close the loop before the next quarter

The audit closes when the queue agrees with the payer records and every exception has a next action. Produce a one-page summary with counts by expiration window, unresolved exceptions, and the oldest open item. Give the summary to the practice administrator and keep it beside the next quarter's audit so the comparison means something. A count that nobody reviews is not a control.

Assign the follow-up work with dates rather than priorities. A high-priority label does not tell a coordinator what to do on Thursday morning. A dated task that names the payer portal, the reference number, and the expected response does. Confirm that the person who received the task has access to the portal before the audit meeting ends.

Schedule the next audit before the quarter gets busy. The best time is usually two weeks before the next quarter boundary, when the schedule is visible but not yet full. Add the review to the practice calendar and name a backup reviewer. The audit becomes a routine only when the next date exists before the current one is finished.
