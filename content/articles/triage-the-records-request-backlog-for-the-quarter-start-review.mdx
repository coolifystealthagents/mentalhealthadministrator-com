## Why the backlog deserves a quarter-start review

A records request backlog grows quietly because each request looks reasonable on its own. One is waiting for a signature, another for a clinician to review, and a third for the front desk to find an old chart. Individually they seem close to finished. Together they can exceed the practice's response target and create a compliance problem before anyone notices.

Quarter start is a useful moment to look because the previous quarter's requests should be closed or explained. New staff may have inherited a folder without context, and a process change may have left older items in the wrong queue. The review should produce a current count, a list of overdue items, and a clear owner for each. A backlog that is counted but not assigned is just a more organized backlog.

The review also tests whether the practice can tell a routine request from one that needs a legal or clinical decision. A request from a client for their own record is not the same as a subpoena or a request involving a minor's custody arrangement. Sorting by type protects the team from applying a routine release process to a request that needs legal review. It also gives the reviewer a defensible reason for the order in which requests are handled.

## Sort by deadline and authorization

Start with the response deadline, not the arrival order. A request that arrived last week but has a short deadline may need attention before one that has been open for a month. Keep the deadline field calculated from the date the request was received and the applicable rule, and store the calculation source. A deadline typed from memory is hard to defend later.

Next, separate requests with valid authorization from those without. A request that lacks a signed authorization cannot be released, but it still needs a timely response explaining what is missing. The response itself is part of the record. Record the date the practice asked for the missing item and the date the requester replied, if they did.

Use three work states rather than a single open state: ready for review, waiting on requester, and in legal review. Each state needs a different next action and a different owner. When every request sits in open, the team cannot see which ones are actually waiting on the practice. The state should be visible on the queue without opening the request.

## Confirm scope before copying anything

The scope of a request determines how much work it creates and what may be released. A request for the complete record is different from a request for billing statements or session dates. Write the scope in the requester's own words and add a short interpretation reviewed by the person who will prepare the release. If the scope is ambiguous, ask the requester before assembling documents.

Check whether the record includes information about another person. A family therapy note, a joint session, or a payment from a third party may require redaction or a different authorization. The administrative team should flag the issue rather than decide it alone. Route the question to the privacy lead with the specific pages and the reason for concern.

Keep a copy of the released pages and the redaction log together. The log should show what was withheld and the rule that supported the withholding, without reproducing the withheld content. A later reviewer may need to confirm that the release matched the request. Without the log, the practice has to reconstruct the decision from memory.

## Protect legal holds and sensitive requests

Some requests should stop the routine process. A subpoena, a court order, or a request connected to a legal claim needs review by the practice's legal contact before any document is copied. The intake team should recognize the signal and route the item without interpreting the demand. A short list of signals at the intake desk prevents a routine clerk from making a legal decision.

A request that mentions a minor, guardianship, or custody dispute also deserves a pause. The person requesting the record may or may not have the authority to receive it. Check the authorization against the practice's policy and ask for a supervisor when the relationship is unclear. The client's safety and privacy matter more than a fast response in these cases.

Record the escalation date and the person who received the escalation. If the legal review takes time, the requester may still be entitled to an update. Send a short acknowledgment that the practice is reviewing the request and will respond by a stated date. The acknowledgment should not promise a release date the practice cannot meet.

## Measure age, not only volume

A count of open requests says little about whether the backlog is healthy. Measure the age of the oldest open request and the share past the internal target. Those numbers reveal a long tail that an average would hide. A practice with ten requests and one six months old has a different problem from a practice with ten requests that are all five days old.

Break the age down by work state. Requests waiting on the requester are not the same as requests waiting on the practice. Separate them so the team does not get credit for time the practice could not control. The review should focus on the states where the practice is the source of the delay.

Review the returned-request rate as well. A high rate of missing authorization may mean the intake instructions are unclear. A high rate of unclear scope may mean the request form invites vague language. Each rate points to a different fix, and both are more useful than a general note that the team is busy.

## Close with a dated control

End the review with three lists: overdue items, items waiting on the practice, and items waiting on someone else. Give each item on the first two lists an owner and a date. Ask the owner to confirm the next action in the queue rather than in a separate message. The queue should remain the single place where the work is visible.

Choose one intake change to reduce future backlog. It might be a clearer authorization checklist, a required scope field, or an automatic acknowledgment message. Write the change and set a date to check its effect. A small change that is actually implemented beats a comprehensive policy that the team cannot follow.

Keep the review summary with the privacy records and note the reviewer. The next quarter's review should be able to compare counts, ages, and returned-request rates without rebuilding the method. A repeatable record is the difference between a one-time cleanup and a working control. Store it where the privacy lead can find it during an audit.
