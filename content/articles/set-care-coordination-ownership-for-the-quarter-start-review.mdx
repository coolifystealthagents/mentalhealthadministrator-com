## Why ownership drifts in care coordination

Care coordination feels collaborative until a task has no owner. A message goes to a shared inbox, several people read it, and everyone assumes someone else will reply. At quarter start, new schedules and staff changes make that assumption more expensive. A client waits for a referral, a school contact waits for a release, and the practice cannot say who was supposed to act.

The problem is often not effort but ambiguity. A message that says please coordinate with the school does not name the school contact, the question, or the deadline. The reader has to reconstruct the task before deciding whether it belongs to them. A quarter-start review should replace those broad requests with a specific owner and a specific next step.

Care coordination also crosses a boundary the practice must protect. The administrative team can arrange contact, confirm releases, and track responses. It cannot give clinical advice to a school or decide what information should be shared for a clinical purpose. Ownership includes knowing where the administrative role stops and the clinician's role begins.

## Name the next owner in every handoff

Every coordination entry should have one owner at a time. The owner is the person responsible for the next action, even when the action is waiting for an outside reply. If the entry is waiting, the owner still checks the date and follows up. An entry with two owners is effectively unowned because each person can assume the other is watching it.

Write the owner as a person, not a team. A team name helps with routing but does not create accountability. Use the team field for reporting and the person field for the current task. When a person is away, assign a named backup in the same entry rather than leaving the item to the team. The transition should be visible without a separate message.

Include the due date and the question that needs an answer. The question should be concrete enough that a reply can close it. Replace please coordinate with a request such as confirm whether the school can attend a meeting on a named date. A closed question produces a faster response and a cleaner record.

## Keep the open decision visible

Long message threads hide the decision that is still open. A reader has to scroll through updates to find the one question that matters. Put the open decision in a dedicated field at the top of the entry and update it as the situation changes. The thread can hold the history while the field holds the current state.

Close the decision explicitly when it is resolved. Write what was decided, who decided it, and the date. Do not let the entry fade into a general status of in progress after the question has been answered. A resolved decision should leave the active queue and remain available for reference. That separation keeps the active list short and readable.

When a decision changes, add a new dated entry instead of editing the old one. A later reviewer needs to see what was known at the time. If the school contact changed or the release expired, the record should show the sequence. Editing history makes the practice look more certain than it was and can mislead a reviewer.

## Separate updates from clinical instructions

A coordination update tells people what is happening. A clinical instruction tells people what to do about care. The two should not travel in the same message because the recipients have different roles and different authority. Keep the administrative thread focused on logistics, dates, and documents. Route clinical questions to the clinician through the approved channel.

The administrative team can prepare a summary of the coordination status for the clinician. That summary should state the facts and the question, not recommend a clinical action. For example, the team can report that a school asked whether a client may attend a partial day. It should not answer the question on the clinician's behalf. The clinician decides, and the team records the decision.

Use a standard subject line that identifies the client, the coordination type, and the due date. A consistent subject helps the next person find the thread without searching by memory. Avoid putting sensitive detail in the subject because it may appear in notifications. Keep the identifiers internal and the clinical detail in the secured body of the message.

## Review the queue by age and owner

Pull every open coordination item at the start of the quarter and sort by the oldest due date. Look first at items past due, then at items due within the next week. Check that each item has a named owner and a current question. Items that fail either check should be fixed before the team starts working the list.

Group the queue by owner to see whether one person is carrying an unreasonable share. A concentration of items may be a workload issue or a sign that the routing rule sends too much to one role. Compare the counts with the available hours rather than with a headcount. A small team can carry a large queue if most items are waiting on outside replies.

Look for items that have changed owners more than twice. Frequent reassignment can signal an unclear scope or a missing decision. Read those entries and ask whether the question was ever precise enough to answer. A coordination item that keeps moving is often a decision that nobody has authority to make.

## Close with a smaller, clearer queue

End the review by closing items that are actually finished and reassigning items that are not. Remove duplicate entries that refer to the same request. Merge threads that describe the same coordination task so the next owner sees one history. A smaller queue is easier to trust and easier to review next quarter.

Choose one template change and apply it to new entries for the next month. The change could be a required owner field, a required due date, or a standard question format. Measure the share of new entries that meet the template before adding another requirement. Adoption matters more than the elegance of the form.

Share the review result with the clinical team in a short note. Include the number of open items, the oldest item, and the one template change. Ask the clinicians to flag any coordination message that lacks a clear question. Their feedback will reveal the cases the administrative review cannot see.
