## Why the front desk needs a written matrix

The front desk handles the first minute of most difficult calls. A caller may mention a safety concern, a billing dispute, or a records request. The person answering has to decide whether to handle the question, transfer it, or take a message. Without a written matrix, that decision depends on experience and confidence. The practice should keep the clinical question with the clinical owner. The practice should review the wrong-route calls monthly.

A written matrix gives every shift the same starting point. It should name the observable signal, the route, and the person who owns the response. The matrix is not a clinical protocol; it is a routing guide that helps the front desk act without guessing. Write the deadline as a date and a local time. The team should test the script with a real call type. The practice should keep the old form with the change note.

## Name the signals and the routes

Write the signals in plain language that a new receptionist can recognize. A caller who mentions harm, a minor, or a court order needs a different route from a caller who asks about a bill. The signal should be specific enough to apply during a call. Name the owner in the same line as the action. The client's preference should be recorded and followed. The records release should name the authority and the scope.

For each signal, name the primary route and the backup. A route to a department is not enough; the front desk needs a person or a role. Record the expected response time. A short note with four fields is easier to use than a long narrative. The front desk should record the caller's relationship before sharing any detail. The client's preference should be recorded and followed.

## Set the boundary

The front desk should not give clinical advice or promise an outcome. The matrix should say what the receptionist may say, what must be transferred, and what must be documented. A boundary that is not written will be crossed under pressure. Record the exception and the person who approved it. A note that cannot be reconstructed later is not a control. Record the exact language for a transfer. A caller who is transferred without an explanation may call back more upset. The script should be short and respectful. ## Test the matrix with a real call type

Pick one recent call and trace it through the matrix. Check whether the signal matched the route and the owner responded. A mismatch is a finding about the matrix. Write the deadline as a date and a local time. The review should end with one owner and one date. The records release should name the authority and the scope.

Update the matrix after the test. A matrix that is never tested is only an intention. Share the change with the front desk. Record the exception and the person who approved it. ## Review the matrix monthly

Review the calls that reached the wrong route or waited too long. Look for a repeated signal or a missing owner. Update the matrix and the script. Separate what was observed from what was assumed. The record should show the date and the channel of the contact. The schedule change should be checked against the room and the billing.

Keep the review note with the front desk log. A later audit may need to see what changed. The goal is a faster, safer response, not a longer document.
