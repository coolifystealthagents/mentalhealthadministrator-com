A provider can be active in the practice, listed incorrectly by a payer, unavailable for new intake, and open only for a narrow service at the same time. Treating all of those facts as one `open` field creates preventable directory and scheduling errors. A panel audit should reconstruct what changed, where it changed, and which source authorized each update.

## Quick overview:

- Give every panel attribute its own value, source, effective date, and owner.
- Separate practice availability from payer participation and public directory publication.
- Use prospective effective dates so future changes do not appear active early.
- Record conflicts without asking administrative staff to decide contracting or clinical fit.
- Close a change only after all affected destinations are reconciled or explicitly excepted.

## Model panel status as several facts

A single panel flag cannot answer the questions a scheduler actually faces. Build a small status matrix instead:

| Attribute | Example values | Appropriate source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Practice relationship | active, leave, ended | approved workforce record |
| New client intake | open, paused, restricted | authorized operations instruction |
| Payer participation | participating, pending, ended | contract or payer confirmation reviewed by its owner |
| Service scope | adult individual, group only, telehealth only | approved provider profile |
| Location | site A, site B, remote | location assignment |
| Publication state | submitted, published, rejected | directory transaction evidence |

Do not derive one row from another. An active clinician is not necessarily accepting new clients. A payer portal listing is not proof that the practice intended to open a panel. Service or location limits should remain explicit rather than buried in a note.

The [CMS National Plan and Provider Enumeration System](https://nppes.cms.hhs.gov/) is useful for checking NPI data, but it does not replace a practice's payer contract records, availability instruction, or directory confirmation. Label the exact source used instead of calling every outside lookup a credential check.

## Open a change record before editing destinations

Start with a change ID and capture:

- provider ID, without unnecessary client information;
- requested attribute and old and proposed values;
- request source, requester, and received time;
- effective date and, if relevant, end date;
- affected payer, location, service, and scheduling template;
- approval reference and approving role;
- destination checklist with submission and verification states;
- conflict code, owner, and next review date.

The source artifact might be an approved leave instruction, contracting notice, provider profile approval, or payer message. Preserve a reference to the artifact in the approved system. Do not paste contract terms or unrelated personnel details into a general scheduling task.

Prospective dating matters. If intake pauses next Monday, the record may be approved today while the current scheduling status remains unchanged until the effective time. Store both the approved future value and the presently active value. Otherwise, staff may either apply it too soon or overlook it later.

## Reconcile each destination separately

Create a destination map before work begins. It may include the scheduling rule, website profile, payer directories, referral scripts, call center guidance, and an internal roster. For each destination, identify whether the practice can edit directly, must submit a request, or can only report an error.

Use granular progress states such as `not started`, `submitted`, `acknowledged`, `published`, `rejected`, and `verification blocked`. `Submitted` must not be used as a synonym for `updated`. After a portal submission, retain the confirmation number and submission time, then check the displayed result under the practice's approved cadence.

A practical audit compares three things: the approved state effective on the sample date, the value displayed in each destination, and the evidence available to a scheduler on that date. This reveals timing defects that a current-state comparison would miss.

## Route conflicts instead of resolving them by convenience

Suppose the internal intake record says paused, one payer directory says accepting, and a referral spreadsheet says open. The administrator should not choose the majority value. Freeze new edits long enough to identify each source, capture its timestamp, and route the discrepancy to the owners for availability and payer-directory decisions.

Useful exception codes include:

- `effective date unclear`;
- `payer response conflicts with submission`;
- `service scope missing`;
- `provider identity mismatch`;
- `destination has no editable field`;
- `approval withdrawn`;
- `verification unavailable`.

Clinical suitability, licensure interpretation, network contracting, and promises about coverage stay outside routine directory administration. If a message contains a care or safety concern, follow the practice's separate escalation procedure rather than recording it as a panel-status reason.

## Audit a change from request through publication

Select a change ID, not merely a provider. Verify the initiating artifact, authority, effective date, prior state, destination list, transaction evidence, final displayed values, and closure reason. Also check whether edits made after the original request are attributed instead of silently replacing history.

Measure process facts conservatively. Counts of destinations reconciled, unresolved conflicts, rejected submissions, and changes missing an effective date can help owners inspect the control. Report the sample period and exclusions. A clean directory field does not demonstrate access to care, payer payment, or any clinical result.

Review patterns by destination and failure code. Multiple `published incorrectly` cases at one payer may warrant a revised submission procedure. Repeated missing service restrictions may point to a weak intake form. The corrective action should address the observed fault, then be checked on a later sample.

## Decide when the record may close

Closure requires either verified agreement across required destinations or a documented exception owned by a named person. An outside portal that has not updated should remain visible as pending, even if internal systems are correct. If the provider leaves before reconciliation finishes, retain the open directory exception instead of burying it under the workforce closure.

A closure note should state the final effective state, destinations verified, unresolved items, their owners, and the evidence date. This makes the record usable during absence and prevents another administrator from reopening the same investigation from scratch.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a provider's NPI record the authority for panel availability?

No. NPI data and panel availability answer different questions. Use the source assigned to each attribute and record any inconsistency for review.

### Can an administrator mark a payer panel closed after a provider asks?

Only through the practice's approved process and with the required authorization. The administrator may carry out an authorized transaction, but should not interpret a contract or decide network participation.

### What if a payer directory cannot represent the practice's restriction?

Record the field limitation, preserve the submitted wording or confirmation, and send the mismatch to the directory owner. Do not force a misleading value merely to clear the queue.

### When should a panel change be rechecked?

Use the practice's defined schedule, with an additional check after rejected transactions, amended effective dates, provider moves, contract changes, or evidence that a public listing is wrong.

## Close with an attributable conclusion

A defensible panel audit does not aim to make every system look identical without context. It shows which attributes were authorized, when they became effective, what each destination displayed, and who owns any remaining discrepancy.
